# 1. Identity

**3C is a context-focused strategic approach that directs organizational attention to the source, dependency, or constraint most relevant to improving effective application and execution.**

3C is built on a simple premise:

> **Context determines focus.**

The context from which a problem is viewed influences what becomes visible, what question is asked, what insight is produced, and ultimately what deserves attention.

3C therefore does not begin by asking what to improve.

It begins by asking:

> **What context are we looking at?**

---

# 2. Core Proposition

3C proposes that organizational problems are often interpreted too quickly through their visible consequences.

The same visible problem—delay, rework, workload, escalation, approval, coordination, or poor responsiveness—can arise from very different underlying conditions.

Therefore:

> **Context determines focus. Context changes the question. Changed questions create or reveal insight.**

The central intellectual contribution of 3C is not a new way to improve everything.

It is a way to **change the context of attention so that better questions become possible.**

# 4. The Central Distinction

Traditional management often asks:

> **How can we do this work better?**

3C asks:

> **What context is creating or sustaining this condition?**

This shifts attention:

| Conventional Attention | 3C Strategic Attention |
|---|---|
| Improve the activity | Understand the condition |
| Optimize visible work | Examine what creates the work |
| Manage the consequence | Investigate the context |
| Fix the symptom | Question the source, dependency, or constraint |
| Increase effort | Understand what makes effort necessary |
| Add resources | Examine why existing capability cannot be effectively applied |
| Automate activity | Question whether the activity should exist in its current form |
| Manage complexity | Understand what keeps generating complexity |

The shift is from **managing visible consequences** to **understanding the context that creates or sustains them**.

# 5. Foundational Distinction

3C uses three organizational contexts:

| Context | Strategic Focus | Foundational Logic |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Continuous Improvement | **SOURCE → IMPROVE** |
| **Dependency** | Continuous Empowerment | **DEPENDENCY → EMPOWER** |
| **Constraint** | Continuous Constraint Reduction | **CONSTRAINT → REDUCE** |

These are **contexts for attention**, not mandatory sequential stages.

A condition may primarily require one context, while another may require a combination.

---

# 6. The Three Contexts

## 6.1 Source → Continuous Improvement

The **Source** context asks what keeps generating recurring demand, friction, rework, exceptions, or unnecessary activity.

### Core question

> **Why does this keep arising?**

Examples include:

- repeated errors,
- recurring requests,
- duplicated work,
- recurring exceptions,
- repeated corrections,
- avoidable rework.

### Example

A billing team repeatedly receives correction requests.

Instead of simply processing corrections faster, the Source context asks:

> **Why does the billing error keep arising?**

The objective is to understand and improve the condition at its source.

---

## 6.2 Dependency → Continuous Empowerment

The **Dependency** context asks why action repeatedly depends on another person, function, system, approval, or intervention.

### Core question

> **Why does action depend on someone or something else?**

Examples include:

- routine approvals,
- repeated escalations,
- unnecessary handoffs,
- managerial intervention,
- dependency on specialist functions,
- repeated requests for information already available elsewhere.

### Example

Routine customer requests require managerial approval even when employees have the necessary knowledge and authority to resolve them.

The Dependency context asks:

> **Why is approval required for this action?**

The answer may reveal an opportunity for appropriate empowerment.

Continuous Empowerment does not mean unlimited autonomy.

It means reducing **unnecessary dependency** where capability, information, authority, accountability, and risk conditions permit.

---

## 6.3 Constraint → Continuous Constraint Reduction

The **Constraint** context asks what condition is restricting action, flow, responsiveness, or effective application.

### Core question

> **What is preventing effective action?**

Examples include:

- system limitations,
- restrictive workflow design,
- bottlenecks,
- policy barriers,
- unavailable information,
- unnecessary controls,
- conflicting requirements.

### Example

An employee has the capability to resolve a customer issue but cannot do so because the system does not permit the required action.

The Constraint context asks:

> **What is preventing effective action?**

The objective is not to remove every constraint.

Necessary governance, safety, quality, compliance, risk, and control conditions may remain.

The objective is to reduce **unnecessary or disproportionate constraints** that restrict effective application.

# 7. Context Determines Focus

The same visible problem can require different questions depending on context.

For example, consider **repeated approvals**.

The visible activity is approval.

But the relevant context might be:

- **Source:** Why does this type of request keep arising?
- **Dependency:** Why does action require approval?
- **Constraint:** What rule or system prevents direct resolution?

Therefore:

> **The activity does not determine the question. The context determines the question.**

This is central to 3C.

A change in context changes what management considers relevant.

```text

       ↓

       ↓
ASK DIFFERENT QUESTION
       ↓
```

The purpose is not to ask more questions.

It is to ask **better questions because the context has changed**.

| Rework | **Source** | What keeps generating the rework? |
| Repeated approvals | **Dependency** | Why does action require approval? |
| Escalations | **Dependency** | Why can't action occur at the appropriate level? |
| Handoffs | **Dependency** | Why does the work depend on another party? |
| Bottlenecks | **Constraint** | What is restricting flow? |
| System barriers | **Constraint** | What is preventing effective action? |
| Policy barriers | **Constraint** | What condition is unnecessarily restricting action? |
| Slow response | **Source / Dependency / Constraint** | What context best explains the restriction? |

The purpose of the guide is **not classification for its own sake**.

The purpose is to identify the context that produces the most useful question.

3C uses contextual inquiry rather than assuming that every visible problem has the same cause.

A basic inquiry sequence is:

```text
WHAT DO WE OBSERVE?
        ↓
WHAT CONTEXT BEST EXPLAINS IT?
        ↓
WHAT DOES THAT CONTEXT MAKE VISIBLE?
        ↓
WHAT QUESTION DOES IT REVEAL?
        ↓
WHAT INSIGHT DOES THE QUESTION PRODUCE?
        ↓
WHAT DESERVES ATTENTION?
```

3C therefore supports diagnosis, but its primary contribution is **strategic attention through contextual inquiry**.

- recurring friction,
- repeated rework,
- unnecessary dependency,
- excessive intervention,
- repeated escalation,

- all work is unnecessary,

The objective is not indiscriminate reduction.

3C and 5R are complementary but distinct.

> **3C = WHERE**  
> **5R = HOW**

3C identifies the relevant context and changes the question.

5R provides a tactical decision hierarchy when the condition involves recurring operational demand that needs to be challenged.

```text
3C

             ↓
      CONTEXT → FOCUS → QUESTION
             ↓
        RELEVANT INSIGHT
             ↓
            5R
             ↓
```

3C does not require 5R in every case.

Likewise, not every 5R decision requires a prior 3C inquiry.

# 19. Core Principles

### Principle 1 — Context Before Intervention

Understand the context before deciding what to change.

### Principle 2 — Question Before Optimization

Do not optimize an activity before asking whether the activity or its recurring demand should exist in its current form.

### Principle 3 — Source Before Symptom

Where recurring conditions exist, examine what keeps generating them.

### Principle 4 — Dependency Before Escalation

Where action repeatedly requires intervention, examine why the dependency exists.

### Principle 5 — Constraint Before Blame

Where capability exists but action is restricted, examine the condition before assuming individual failure.

### Principle 6 — Insight Before Action

Better questions should produce better understanding before intervention is selected.

### Principle 7 — Change Conditions, Not Just Consequences

Where possible, change the condition that repeatedly creates the consequence.

### Principle 8 — Necessary Conditions Must Remain

Do not remove valuable demand, appropriate dependency, or necessary constraints merely because they consume capacity.

### Principle 9 — Capacity Effect Must Be Examined

A changed condition matters because of what it makes possible—not merely because an activity disappeared.

### Principle 10 — Prevent Recurrence

Where a condition can recreate itself, address the mechanism that sustains it.

# 21. Prioritization

3C does not require every identified context to be addressed simultaneously.

A useful prioritization heuristic within the broader HEE logic is:

> **Priority = Urgency × Dependency × Value Impact**

This is a **heuristic, not a universal law**.

A condition may deserve greater attention when:

- its consequence is urgent,
- other work depends on resolving it,
- and changing it has meaningful value implications.

The central rule remains:

> **Do not automatically fix the largest problem. Focus on the condition that most prevents effective application and execution.**

# 23. 3C in Practice

## Example 1 — Repeated Approvals

**Visible condition:** Routine requests repeatedly require management approval.

**Context:** Dependency.

**Question:**

> Why does action depend on approval?

**Potential insight:** Employees may possess the necessary capability, information, and accountability but lack appropriate decision authority.

**Possible action:** Redesign authority boundaries where risk and governance permit.

---

## Example 2 — Repeated Billing Errors

**Visible condition:** Billing corrections repeatedly consume operational capacity.

**Context:** Source.

**Question:**

> Why does the error keep arising?

**Potential insight:** The correction process may be treating the symptom while the underlying source remains unchanged.

**Possible action:** Address the recurring source condition.

---

## Example 3 — Workflow Bottleneck

**Visible condition:** Work repeatedly accumulates at one stage.

**Context:** Constraint.

**Question:**

> What is preventing effective flow?

**Potential insight:** The bottleneck may result from system design, policy, sequencing, capacity access, or another restrictive condition.

**Possible action:** Reduce or redesign the relevant constraint where appropriate.

---

## Example 4 — Competing Strategic Priorities

**Visible condition:** Leadership repeatedly launches initiatives, but execution becomes fragmented and priorities compete for attention.

**Possible context:** Source, Dependency, or Constraint.

**Questions:**

> **Source:** Why does the organization keep generating new priorities without retiring existing ones?

> **Dependency:** Why do strategic decisions require multiple competing sponsors or repeated alignment cycles?

> **Constraint:** What governance or structural condition prevents meaningful prioritization?

**Potential insight:** The visible problem may not simply be weak prioritization discipline. The organization may be continually generating, distributing, or sustaining competing demands through its own structural conditions.

**Possible action:** Change the condition that continually recreates strategic fragmentation.

This example demonstrates that 3C applies not only to frontline operations but also to **strategic organizational conditions**.

# 34. Boundary Principles

3C does not mean:

- improve everything,
- empower everyone,
- remove every dependency,
- remove every constraint,
- eliminate approvals,
- eliminate governance,
- eliminate management,
- automate every activity,
- reduce every form of work,
- maximize autonomy,
- maximize speed.

Instead:

> **Reduce unnecessary conditions while preserving necessary conditions.**

Therefore:

- necessary demand should be retained,
- appropriate dependency should be governed,
- necessary constraints should be protected,
- appropriate controls should remain,
- capability should be matched with authority and accountability,
- interventions should be proportionate to the condition.

The objective is **not minimum activity**.

The objective is **appropriate conditions for effective application and sustainable execution**.

> **Condition Change → Capacity Effect → Effective Application → Execution → Sustainable Execution Capacity → Sustainable Business Value**

### Leadership Test

> What context are we looking at?  
> What question does it reveal?  
> What insight does it produce?  
> What deserves attention now?  
> What should change?  
> What could go wrong if we do not?  
> What becomes possible if it changes?

### Simplest Form

> **Context determines focus.  
> Focus changes the question.  
> Better questions reveal better insight.  
> Better insight directs better attention.  
> Better attention enables better change.**

### Final Principle

> **Do not begin by asking what to improve.  
> Begin by understanding the context that determines what question should be asked.**

### Final Question

> **What context are we looking at—and what question does it make possible?**
